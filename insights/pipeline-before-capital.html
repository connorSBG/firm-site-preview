Pipeline before capital: how to think about a university venture fund

A venture fund is the most visible thing an institution can build around entrepreneurship, and it works best with a pipeline behind it. A way to sequence programs and capital, the options short of a fund, and the signals that a fund is the right move.

690 words, about 3 min
DraftWorking draft under review. Figures not yet confirmed are tagged for verification.

A venture fund is the most legible thing an institution can build around entrepreneurship. A trustee understands it, a donor can picture it, and a president can announce it. The quieter work that produces ventures, the fellowship, the competition, the microgrants, the IP policy, rarely gets a press release. So the fund is often the first thing proposed, ahead of the programs that would produce the companies it invests in.

The principle we design around is simple: pipeline before capital. A fund needs ventures to invest in, and ventures come from programs.

The count that decides it

A fund that plans to make eight to twelve investments over three years needs a steady flow of investable companies. At many institutions the current annual count is somewhere between zero and a handful. That is not a reason to give up on capital. It is a reason to sequence it.

Our partners designed and co-founded a university-affiliated venture fund and built it from the fund structure and the partnership agreement through fundraising and deployment. The programs that fed it were launched first and ran alongside it. We are not against funds. We are for funds with something to invest in.

What to build first, in order

Something that finds founders. An idea challenge, a course built on Disciplined Entrepreneurship, a high school pipeline program, an alumni founder survey. The survey alone usually changes the conversation: it turns up ventures that had not yet been counted.

Something that funds them a little. Microgrants at the earliest stages, a pitch competition with a sponsored prize pool, a proof-of-concept fund for faculty inventions. Small money, placed deliberately, moves ideas further than most people expect.

Something that builds them. A fellowship with requirements and tracking, a pre-accelerator, mentors, and an IP policy a student can read in ten minutes. This is where ventures become investable.

Then, when the count supports it, something that invests.

The options short of a fund

This list rarely appears in a fund proposal, because the people who present fund proposals usually want to run the fund.

  • A microgrant pool. Donor-funded, no securities, no manager, no returns expected. The simplest way to put money into ideas.
  • A student-managed fund. Small checks, a teaching mandate, a faculty advisor. Builds the pipeline and the skills at the same time.
  • A sidecar. Co-invest alongside a regional fund that already exists, on deals they source. Exposure without an operation.
  • An LP commitment. Institutional or foundation money into someone else’s fund, with negotiated access: a committee observer seat, dealflow sharing, student involvement.
  • A fund-of-one. An outside manager runs a vehicle with your capital and your mandate. Closer to a fund, without becoming a fund operator.
  • An independent affiliated fund. The full version. Right when the pipeline and the partnership terms are both right.

The signals that a fund is right

Build the fund when all of these hold. Founders are coming out of your programs every year and leaving to raise money elsewhere. Leadership wants to participate in upside, not only to support founders. A credible outside manager will raise and run it, so the institution is never the fund operator. And the partnership terms are negotiable: a share of carried interest back to the institution, seats on a joint investment committee, clear rules on exclusivity and donor access, and a student sub-fund if teaching is part of the point.

That structure is the part of the fund our partners co-founded that transfers to other institutions. The fund’s own metrics are its to publish. VERIFY: capital raised, number of investments, portfolio follow-on funding

Why the advice is only about you

We do not manage capital. We design the structure and write the decision memo, and we have no stake in whether the answer is yes. Ask anyone advising you on a fund what they gain if you start one. Then ask what they gain if you do not.

If the answer is “not yet,” that is a good outcome. It means the next eighteen months go to building the thing that will make the fund work.
