Founder-friendly IP policy, in plain English

What a founder-friendly intellectual property policy says, why disclosures rise when the policy is readable, and what counsel still has to review.

629 words, about 3 min
DraftWorking draft under review. Figures not yet confirmed are tagged for verification.

A student founder should be able to say in one sentence who owns the idea they built in class. A faculty member should know what happens after an invention disclosure, and why filing one is worth the time.

Where that is not yet true, the usual reason is not secrecy. It is a policy written for a different kind of institution, hard to read, with nothing on the other side of a disclosure worth the effort. A founder-friendly policy fixes all three. Here is what it says.

Students own what they make in class

Unless the institution paid for it with sponsored research funds, provided significant resources beyond what any student receives, or employed the student to make it, the student owns it. Say that in one sentence, early, and campus culture changes. Students bring projects to the competition instead of keeping them quiet.

Significant resources means something specific: a lab with a dedicated technician, a sponsored research grant, a confidential dataset. It does not mean a library, a classroom, a makerspace printer, or a faculty member’s office hours. Write the list.

Faculty inventions get a standard deal, fast

The institution typically owns inventions made with institutional resources or sponsored funding. What founders need is predictability: a standard revenue share, a standard license, and a decision within a defined number of days.

A founder-friendly policy sets the inventor’s share high enough to be worth the paperwork, publishes a standard license term sheet so most licenses are signed without negotiation, and commits to a triage decision on a calendar. If the institution will not pursue an invention, it says so quickly and returns the rights to the inventor on standard terms. VERIFY: the specific revenue-share split and decision timeline to publish

The spinout path is written down

When a faculty or student founder wants to build a company around institutional IP, there should be a known path: a standard license to the founder’s company with modest equity or royalty terms, a conflict-of-interest process that lets the founder stay on faculty, and a clear statement of what the institution will and will not take. Many institutions are better served by a small equity stake and an engaged founder than by royalty terms that constrain the company.

Disclosure is a form, not a filing

A disclosure should take ten minutes. Name, idea, what it does, who was involved, what funding touched it. The triage committee does the rest. A form that takes an afternoon and asks for claims language goes unfilled.

What happens after disclosure

A triage process with three outcomes: pursue (file, license, or spin out), release (return it to the inventor), or hold (wait for more data, with a date). Every outcome has an owner and a deadline. A proof-of-concept fund, even a small one, gives “pursue” somewhere to go.

Outside help, chosen on purpose

Many institutions should not build a tech transfer office. A policy can say that the institution uses a university or commercial partner for patent and licensing work, and set criteria for choosing one: cost structure, speed, founder-friendliness, and the share of revenue the partner takes. Choosing deliberately is different from drifting into an arrangement.

What counsel still does

Everything above is design. Counsel reviews the policy for consistency with employment agreements, sponsored research terms, state law, and federal requirements for inventions made with federal funds. The governance process adopts it. We write the plain-English version and the term sheet and coordinate with your counsel so the legal version matches.

The measure of success: disclosures go up, licenses close in days instead of months, and student founders can answer the ownership question in one sentence. At that point the IP policy stops being a document and becomes a pipeline.
