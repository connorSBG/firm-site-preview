Sizing the ask: how to build a gift menu for entrepreneurship programs

Donors give to things with names and numbers. How to build a gift menu for entrepreneurship programs, what the amounts should be anchored to, and the mistakes that leave money on the table.

647 words, about 3 min
DraftWorking draft under review. Figures not yet confirmed are tagged for verification.

An alumni founder calls the dean and says, “I want to support what you are doing with entrepreneurship. What do you need?” The answer you want to give is a list with names and numbers on it. The call lasts a few minutes, and the list has to exist before it comes.

Entrepreneurship programs are easy to give to and hard to ask for until the gifts are sized. A gift menu does that. Here is how to build one.

Start from the budget, not the donor

Every program on the menu needs a real annual cost: prizes, stipends, staff time, food, travel, software, space. A microgrant program that gives twelve awards of $2,500 costs $30,000 in awards plus review time. A pitch competition costs its prize pool plus the event. A fellowship with twenty students and a modest stipend has a number. Write them all down before the first conversation.

Every major gift to the center on our Results page began as a line on a menu with a cost next to it: the sponsored prize pool, the donor-funded microgrants and pre-accelerator, the seven-figure naming gift.

Three kinds of asks

Annual program sponsorship. A donor or corporate sponsor funds a program for a year, usually at the program’s cost plus a margin for staff time. The easiest asks and the most renewable. The sponsor’s name goes on the program, with a seat at the showcase and a report.

Named funds. A multi-year or endowed gift that funds a program in perpetuity, sized so the annual payout covers the cost. At a payout around four to five percent, a program that costs $30,000 a year needs roughly a $600,000 to $750,000 endowment. VERIFY: your institution’s endowment payout rate Do the arithmetic on the menu so the donor does not have to.

Naming opportunities. Spaces, programs, fellowships, and competitions that can carry a name, at minimum gift levels set by the institution’s naming policy. The schedule lists each one with its minimum.

Anchor every amount

Donors can tell when a number is invented. Anchor every amount to something real: the program’s cost, the naming policy, the endowment payout, or what a peer institution charges for the same sponsorship. Where the anchor does not exist yet, go find it before you ask.

Corporate sponsors want a package

A bank sponsoring a pitch competition is buying something: visibility with students, a relationship with the institution, judges’ seats for its executives, a story for its community report. A sponsor package lists levels, benefits at each level, and prices. Treat it as a product. Renewal rates rise when the sponsor knows exactly what it received.

Mistakes that leave money on the table

Asking for operating support instead of a program. “Support the center” is vague. “Fund the fellowship for three years” is a thing a donor can picture and own.

No named opportunities ready when a gift appears. The naming schedule should exist before the first donor conversation.

Different numbers in different places. The cost on the gift menu, the cost in the budget, and the cost in the impact report must match. Donors read all three.

No impact report. A donor who funded microgrants wants to know what the grants produced: projects, students, follow-on funding, a venture or two. The report is the next ask.

Forgetting grants. Federal and state programs fund planning, capacity, and commercialization. A grant readiness map, listing what each program requires and when it opens, lets the advancement office combine a donor’s gift with public money.

Where the work divides

Your advancement team makes the asks. We build the case for support, the gift and sponsorship menu, the naming schedule, the sponsor packages, the grant readiness map, and the impact report template, so that the next time an alumni founder calls and asks what you need, the answer is a document.
