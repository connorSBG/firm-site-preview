A front door for startups and clinicians: the pilot program that comes before a venture arm

Whether your health system needs a venture arm is a fair question. The more useful first move is a front door: a pilot process with a standard agreement, a clinician invention pathway, and a venture decision made on evidence.

648 words, about 3 min
DraftWorking draft under review. Figures not yet confirmed are tagged for verification.

The largest forty US health systems report a median innovation budget around $3.5 million and about 9.5 full-time staff. VERIFY: source for the forty-system median budget and staffing Most systems are not going to replicate that, and do not need to. But the board has read about venture arms, the chief medical officer keeps hearing about clinician inventions, and startups are already piloting products somewhere in the system.

The useful first move is not a fund. It is a front door.

What is already happening

Walk the halls and you find it. A nurse manager built a workflow tool that three units now use. A surgeon designed a device and is talking to a manufacturer. A startup is piloting a scheduling product in one clinic at a department chair’s invitation. IT joined when the data question came up, and procurement will join at the end.

None of this is bad. All of it is better with a tracker, a metric, and a standard agreement. A successful pilot needs a path to a contract so it ends in a decision. A clinician’s invention stays with the system when there is a policy the clinician can read.

The pilot program

A pilot and sandbox program is a process, not a space. It has five parts.

Problem intake. Department leaders state the problems they would pay to solve. This reverses the usual pattern: instead of startups pitching products, the system says what it needs.

A reverse pitch. The system presents its problems to the market and startups respond. Selection is scored against the stated problem, not the quality of the pitch deck.

A standard pilot agreement. One template, pre-cleared with compliance, IT, legal, and clinical leadership, covering data, access, duration, and what happens to the results. A pilot that took months to paper can start in weeks.

Success metrics, set up front. What the pilot has to show, in what time, for the system to buy. Written before the pilot starts, so the end is a decision.

A pilot-to-contract gate. Procurement is involved from the start. A successful pilot has a known next step and a known owner.

Add a tracker that lists every pilot in the system, and an innovation office of one or two people with a steering committee to run it.

The clinician pathway, alongside it

The same office handles clinician inventions: a ten-minute intake form, a triage committee, and a revenue-sharing policy written so a nurse can read it. Most systems should not build in-house tech transfer. They should choose a university or commercial partner for patent and licensing work, with criteria.

When the venture question becomes real

After a year of pilots, the system knows which startups it would buy from again, which it would invest in, and whether its own clinicians are producing anything a fund could back. That is the moment to write the venture decision memo, and there are more options than a fund.

Co-invest alongside a partner in a vendor you already pilot. Commit as an LP to an outside fund and negotiate access. Set up a small fund-of-one with an outside manager. Or decide, deliberately, to hold on capital for another year.

Capital-gated health innovation alliances ask for fund commitments that only the largest systems can justify. A system that joins one before it has a pilot process is paying for dealflow it cannot absorb.

Why this order

Pilots produce evidence. Evidence produces a thesis. A thesis makes a venture decision defensible to a board. In the other order, the fund looks for deals while the pilots run on their own, and the two never meet.

We design the office, the pilot program, the clinician pathway, and the venture decision as one system, sized to your budget. Your team runs the pilots. We do not manage capital, and the venture memo recommends what fits your system.
